## Explained simply

**What this page is.** This page walks you through a small study from the first question to the last answer. You do not need any knowledge of programming or statistics. Every idea is explained in everyday words, and every technical word links to a short explanation.

**The study in one sentence.** We take news and blog articles about the 9/11 attacks and ask whether a computer can tell if an article was published on a conspiracy-oriented website or on a mainstream website. We try four ways of doing this and compare them.

**Every section has the same four parts.**

1. **Explained simply.** The idea of the step and the reasons for every choice we made, with an everyday example. This part stands on its own. If you read only this part of every section, you will understand the whole study.
2. **The code.** The real program that ran, shown in small pieces with a short sentence before each one, followed by the tables and pictures it produced. You can skip it. It is there so that anyone can check what was done.
3. **Report.** The text that goes into our final written report for this step. It is shorter and more formal. Here you find the numbers.
4. **Check yourself.** Questions about the step. The answers are hidden: think first, then click a question to see the answer.

**Glossary links.** The first time a technical word appears in a section, it links to the glossary. Click it to jump to a plain explanation with an example. Use your browser's back button to return.

**How to give feedback.** Feedback is the most useful thing you can give us. Under each section you can write what you did not understand, what seems wrong, and what you would criticise. Please be direct. Good feedback names the sentence or number you mean. Examples: "I do not see why we need a validation set", "this example confused me", "the answer to the second question is not clear". Your comments decide what we improve next.

**A tip for reading.** Read the sections in order. Later sections use ideas from earlier ones. If a result looks surprising, ask yourself whether the way we measured it could be misleading. That is exactly the kind of criticism we are looking for.

## Check yourself

Q: Which part of a section would you read if you want to understand a step but have no interest in code?

Q: You meet a word you do not know. What do you do?
A: Click the linked word to open its glossary entry, which explains it in plain words with an example, then go back.

Q: What makes feedback useful?
A: It names the exact sentence, number or example that is unclear or wrong, and says what you would expect instead.
